I've been following Jeffrey Lewis anti-folk jams since the mid 2000s. It's lo-fi, catchy, emotionally honest, and punk. He's also an accomplished comic artist, publishing memoir comics and contributing chapters to anthologies — including a piece on Tuli Kupferberg (The Fugs) in [*The Beats: A Graphic History*](https://bookshop.org/p/books/the-beats-a-graphic-history-harvey-pekar/ce55bfa1d8b3ca7e), which he researched by interviewing Tuli in person. The two sides of his creative output both get showcased at his shows.

This was the second time I caught him live. He's a full DIYer in the most literal way: no tour bus, no hotel. Nope, he asks fans to put him up for the night at each stop on a tour. There's something endearing about that committment to couchsurfing.

He did a Yiddish-influenced song — rhyming, rhythmic, leaning into the New York accent with obvious delight. He's been doing this long enough to have his own vernacular.

{% image "img/IMG_2218.jpeg", "Jeffrey Lewis holding up a large hand-drawn comic book titled 1976-1977 Pittsburgh, performing one of his non-fiction comic songs", "The non-fiction songs are something else" %}

The comic performance the crowd picked was on the Cuban Missile Crisis. He hauls out his oversized comic — large hand-drawn panels held up one at a time as the song moves through the crisis: Turkey, Cuba, October 14th, 1962. It's funny and tense and completely unique.
